# 101 Really Good Pickleball Rules Questions (with Answers)

Every answer below was checked, rule by rule, against the **2026 USA Pickleball
Official Rulebook** (the PDF published at
`usapickleball.org/docs/rules/USAP-Official-Rulebook.pdf`, read on
October 6, 2026). Rule numbers in parentheses are that edition's numbers, so you
can look up the exact wording yourself.

**How to read this**

- Answers are paraphrased, not copied. The rulebook is copyrighted by USA
  Pickleball and is the only authority; if this page and the rulebook ever
  disagree, the rulebook wins.
- Parts I and II of the rulebook apply to all play. Items tagged **(Tournament)**
  come from Part III and apply to tournament play; casual groups may or may not
  adopt them. USA Pickleball encourages non-sanctioned events to follow the
  rulebook, but their directors may be flexible.
- Part IV (wheelchair, adaptive standing and hybrid play) is covered only briefly
  in the last section.
- This page is not an official USA Pickleball document. Nothing here is a
  sanctioned-event claim.

## The court and equipment

**1. How big is a pickleball court?**
20 feet wide and 44 feet long, the same for singles and doubles. (3.A.1)

**2. Where are the court measurements taken, and how wide are the lines?**
Measurements run to the *outside edge* of the perimeter and non-volley zone lines.
Lines should be 2 inches wide and all one color that contrasts with the court and
surroundings. (3.A.2, 3.A.4.e)

**3. How much room is needed around the court?**
The minimum playing surface is 30 by 60 feet. Recommended sizes are 34 by 64 feet
for new construction and tournaments, and 50 by 80 feet for a stadium court.
(3.A.3)

**4. How deep is the non-volley zone ("kitchen")?**
7 feet from the net on each side, spanning the full 20-foot width. All of the
lines bounding it are part of the zone. (3.A.4.c)

**5. How high is the net?**
36 inches at the sidelines and 34 inches at the center, each give or take a
quarter inch. If there is a center strap, the center measurement includes the
strap. (3.B.6, 3.B.7)

**6. What are the other net requirements?**
Any mesh fabric a ball cannot pass through. Posts about 22 feet apart (inside to
inside, plus or minus an inch) and no more than 3 inches across. The net should be
at least 21 feet 9 inches long and at least 30 inches tall, topped with 2-inch
white tape binding over a cord or cable. (3.B.1–3.B.4)

**7. What makes a ball legal?**
Its design must be approved by USA Pickleball. It must be one uniform color
(identification markings aside), carry a manufacturer or supplier name or logo, and
be durable, smooth-molded and free of texturing, though a slight seam ridge is fine.
Any approved ball may be used indoors or outdoors. (3.C.1–3.C.5)

**8. Is there a limit on paddle size, weight or thickness?**
Length plus width, counting any edge guard and butt cap, may not exceed 24 inches,
and the length may not exceed 17 inches. There is no limit on weight or thickness.
(3.D.2, 3.D.3)

**9. What may *not* be on a paddle's hitting surface?**
Holes, cracks, delamination or dents that break the skin; rough texturing; sandpaper
characteristics; natural or synthetic rubber; moving parts that add head momentum;
anti-skid or spin-adding paint or materials; anything else that lets a player put
excessive spin on the ball; and a reflective finish that could bother opponents.
(3.D.5)

**10. What can I change on my own paddle?**
Only: edge-guard tape, weighted tape, manufacturer-integrated weights, manufacturer
interchangeable grips or faces, name decals or ID markings, grip size inserts, grip
wrap, and handwritten pen ID (name, phone, email) or an autograph. Decals and tape
may not reach more than 1 inch above the top of the grip or more than half an inch
in from the edge. For sanctioned play the paddle must be on the USA Pickleball
Approved Paddle List. (3.D.1, 3.D.6, 3.D.7) **(Tournament)** A non-approved paddle
found before a match means switching with no penalty; found *during* a match, it is
a match forfeit; found after the match, the result stands. (18.A)

## Scoring and winning

**11. How are points scored in standard pickleball?**
Only the serving side can score, by winning the rally. (4.A)

**12. How many points win a game?**
The rulebook says a game is typically played to 11 points with a lead of at least
2. Tournament scoring options include games to 11, 15 or 21. (Section 1, 15.C.1)

**13. Do I have to win by two?**
Every tournament scoring option is win-by-two, except team play, which may allow
win-by-one. For everyday play the rulebook describes 11 and a two-point lead as the
typical game. (15.C, Section 1)

**14. What is rally scoring?**
A provisional option where the side that wins *each* rally scores, whoever served.
The score is called as two numbers, and in doubles only one player per side serves
before a side out. (14.A.2–14.A.5)

**15. Is rally scoring official?**
It is a *provisionally approved* option. In tournaments the director may use it,
except in doubles double-elimination events, 2026 USA Pickleball Golden Ticket
events and the 2026 National Championships. (Section 1, 15.C.2) **(Tournament)**

**16. What does a technical foul do to the score?**
It deducts one point from the offender. If the offender is at zero, one point is
added to the opponent instead. It does not change the server. (22.E) **(Tournament)**

**17. What if that adjustment ends the game?**
It still counts, regardless of who was serving in the previous rally. (22.E.1)
**(Tournament)**

**18. When do players change ends in a single-game match?**
When the first side reaches 6 in a game to 11, 8 in a game to 15, or 11 in a game
to 21. The break is at most one minute and the same server continues. (21.B)
**(Tournament)**

## Player positions and the serving sequence

**19. In singles, which side do I serve from?**
The right side when your score is zero or even, the left when it is odd, serving
diagonally to the receiver's matching court. (5.A.1)

**20. How long does a singles server keep serving?**
Until losing a rally, alternating sides after each point won. Losing the rally is a
side out. (5.A.2, 5.A.3)

**21. In doubles, how many players serve before a side out?**
Both partners, except at the start of each game when only the starting server
serves. (5.B)

**22. Why does a team serve only once at the start of a game?**
The starting server is designated the *second* server for the team's first turn, so
losing that first rally is a side out. That's why the game starts at
"zero – zero – two." (5.B.2, 6.B.2)

**23. Who are the "first server" and "second server"?**
After each side out, whoever is correctly on the right side for the team's score
starts as the first server; their partner is the second server. When the first
server loses a rally, serve passes to the second server; when the second server
loses one, it is a side out. (5.B.2, 5.B.5, 5.B.6)

**24. Which side should each partner stand on?**
The starting server is on the right when their team's score is zero or even and on
the left when it is odd. Their partner takes the opposite side. The server
alternates sides after each point the team wins. (5.B.3, 5.B.5)

**25. Where may I stand during a rally?**
Anywhere, on or off the court, as long as you are on your own end of the net while
the ball is live. Positions are only enforced for the server and receiver. The only
net-crossing allowed is the follow-through on your own hit. (5.B.4, 13.I)

**26. May I ask who should be serving or receiving?**
Yes. Before the serve is hit you may ask opponents who the correct server or
receiver is, or whether positions are right, and they must answer. (5.C)

**27. What if someone notices a server or position error mid-rally?**
Any player may stop the rally. If the claim is correct (and the correct server or
receiver was out of position when the score was called), the rally is replayed. If
the claim is wrong, it is a fault against whoever stopped play. (5.C.1.a, 5.C.1.b)

**28. What if the wrong player returns the serve?**
If the correct server and receiver were both in position when the score was called,
the wrong returner commits a fault, even if the rally finishes. It must be called
before the next serve. (5.C.1.c)

**29. What if the error is only noticed after the rally?**
The result of the rally stands. (5.C.2)

## Readiness and calling the score

**30. Who calls the score and in what form?**
The server normally does. Singles: two numbers, server's then receiver's, starting
"zero – zero." Doubles: three numbers: serving team's score, receiving team's
score, then the server number, starting "zero – zero – two." (6.B, 6.B.1, 6.B.2)

**31. May my partner call the score?**
Yes, if the server cannot, and the same player must call it for the whole game
unless their voice becomes impaired. (6.B.2.a)

**32. When can the serve be hit?**
Only after the *entire* score has been called. Serving mid-call is a fault against
the server. (6.C, 6.C.1)

**33. How long do I have to serve?**
10 seconds after the score call is finished, or it is a fault. (6.D, 6.D.1)

**34. What if the serving team changes serving areas after the score is called?**
The server must let everyone reposition and then re-call the score, which restarts
the 10-second count. (6.D.2; with a referee, 20.D)

**35. How do I signal "not ready"?**
Before the score call starts: raise the paddle above your head, raise your
non-paddle hand above your head, or turn your back to the net completely. After the
score call starts, these signals are not valid while the ball is live, except to
indicate a hinder. (6.A.1, 6.A.2)

**36. Can I challenge a score call?**
Before the return of serve is hit and before the ball is dead. If the score really
was wrong, replay with the correct score. If it was right, it is a fault against the
challenger. After the return is hit, a challenge is also a fault. (6.F.1–6.F.3)

**37. What if a wrong score is only spotted after the rally?**
The rally result stands and the score is corrected before the next serve. (6.F.4)

## Serving

**38. Which serves are legal?**
The volley serve (hit out of the air) and the drop serve (ball bounces first). (7.C,
7.D)

**39. What are the requirements for a volley serve?**
At contact, the paddle must be moving in a clear upward arc, the highest point of
the paddle head must clearly not be above the highest part of the wrist joint, and
the ball must clearly be no higher than the waist. Forehand or backhand is fine.
Breaking any of the first three is a fault. (7.C.1–7.C.5)

**40. What are the requirements for a drop serve?**
Release the ball from a natural, unaided height without propelling it, let it
bounce, then hit it. There is no limit on how many bounces or where it bounces
before you hit it. Forehand or backhand is fine. (7.D.1–7.D.5)

**41. May I throw the ball down or push it to bounce higher?**
No. Releasing from an aided height or adding force to the drop is an illegal drop
serve, a fault. (7.D.1, 7.D.2, 7.D.6)

**42. Can I spin the ball before I hit it?**
No. You may not manipulate the ball or add spin with your body or paddle during
release, though letting it roll off the paddle by gravity is allowed. Spin applied
by the paddle at contact is fine. The receiver may call a replay for suspected
manipulation, but only before returning the serve. (7.B.2, 7.B.2.a)

**43. How must the ball be released?**
Using one hand or the paddle only, and the receiver must be able to see the release.
If they can't, they may call for a replay before returning the serve. (7.B.1, 7.B.3,
7.B.3.a)

**44. May I jump when I serve?**
No. If both of your feet are off the playing surface when the serve is hit, it is a
fault. At least one foot must be in contact with the correct serving area. (7.A.1,
7.A.1.a)

**45. Where must my feet be at contact?**
Neither foot may touch the court, and neither may touch the playing surface outside
the correct serving area. The court is measured to the outside edge of the lines,
so a foot on the baseline counts as touching the court. The serving area is behind
the baseline between the imaginary extensions of the sideline and centerline. (7.A.2,
7.A.3, 3.A.2, 3.A.4.g)

**46. Where must the serve land?**
In the service court diagonally opposite your correct position, clearing the
opponent's non-volley zone. Landing out of bounds or in the non-volley zone is a
fault. (7.E, 7.E.1, 7.E.2)

**47. Does a serve that lands on a line count?**
On the baseline, sideline or centerline of the correct service court: yes, those
lines are part of the service court. On the non-volley zone line: no, it is out,
because the line is part of the zone. (3.A.4.f, 8.B, 8.C, 7.E.2)

**48. What if the serve clips the net and lands in?**
It is good and play continues. A serve may touch the net on the way over, provided
it lands in the correct service court. (7.E, 13.A)

**49. What if the serve hits someone or something else?**
A serve that touches a permanent object (such as a wall) or the net posts or other
net support before landing is a fault against the server. One that hits the server,
the server's partner, or anything either wears or carries is a fault against the
server. One that hits the receiver, the receiver's partner, or anything they wear or
carry before landing is a fault against the *receiver*. (7.E.3–7.E.5, 13.B)

## Line calls and fair play

**50. Who makes line calls?**
In everyday play, players call "out" for all lines on their own end, and either
partner may make the call. (8.A)

**51. What is "in" and what is "out"?**
A serve that clears the non-volley zone line and lands in the correct service court
is in; a returned ball that lands on the opponent's end is in. A serve that misses
the correct service court (including a landing on the non-volley zone line) is out,
and any other ball landing outside the court is out. (8.B, 8.C)

**52. What if I'm not sure?**
The opponent gets the benefit of the doubt. A ball that cannot be promptly called
"out" is in. (8.D)

**53. How certain must I be to call a ball out?**
You must clearly see space between the line and the ball when it lands. (8.E)

**54. When and how must an "out" call be made?**
Promptly, by voice, hand signal or both. If you returned the ball, call it before
your opponent hits it or before the ball is dead, otherwise play continues. If you
did not return it, a prompt call is still recognized even if the ball has already
become dead. A line call stops play. (8.F, 8.F.2, 8.F.3)

**55. What if my partner and I disagree on a call?**
The call is "in." (8.H)

**56. Can I take back a call?**
You may override your own call, your partner's call or an "in" ruling, but only to
your own disadvantage. (8.G)

**57. Can I ask my opponents what they saw?**
Yes, until the next serve is hit. A clear "in" or "out" from them stands. If they
don't give a definite call, your call stands, or the ball is "in" if you made none.
Once you ask, you and your partner lose the right to make that call, except to
override in the opponent's favor. (8.I)

**58. Can spectators decide a disputed call?**
No. Spectators must not be consulted on any call. (8.J)

## Faults, dead balls and hinders

**59. What is a fault?**
A rules violation that makes the ball dead and loses the rally. (Definitions, 9.B)

**60. May I stop play whenever I think something went wrong?**
Only for specific reasons. Stopping a rally is itself a fault against you, except to
call a hinder, to correctly identify a server or position error, or to correct a
wrong score before the return of serve. (9.A.1)

**61. Who calls faults?**
Players call faults on themselves and their partner as soon as they are committed or
noticed. (9.B.2)

**62. Which faults may I call on my opponents?**
Only non-volley zone faults and service foot faults. Call them as soon as they are
detected and before the next serve is hit. If the teams disagree, replay the rally.
If partners disagree, the benefit goes to their opponents. (9.B.3)

**63. What about other faults I think my opponents committed?**
You may mention the specific fault after the rally, but you cannot enforce it. The
final decision belongs to the player who allegedly committed it. (9.B.4)

**64. What is a hinder, and what is not one?**
A hinder is a passing outside interruption not caused by a player, such as a stray
ball, a flying insect, foreign material, or players or officials on another court,
that actually interferes with your ability to play the ball. Any player may call it
and the rally is replayed. Permanent objects like walls, ceilings, fencing, lights
and net posts are never hinders. (Definitions, 9.C.1, 9.C.2) **(Tournament)** With a
referee, they must validate the hinder; an invalid one is a fault against the
caller. (20.H)

**65. What does "replay" mean?**
The rally restarts with no point awarded and no change of server. (Definitions)

## Rally situations

**66. What is the two-bounce rule?**
The serve must bounce before it is returned, and then the return of serve must
bounce before the serving side hits it. Volleying the serve (receiver) or the return
(serving side) is a fault. (10.A, 10.A.1, 10.A.2)

**67. How many bounces may the ball take before I return it?**
One. If you fail to return a ball before its second bounce, it is a fault against
you. (10.B, 10.B.1)

**68. When does my own shot lose the rally?**
When it first lands outside the opponent's end, or when it doesn't cross the net and
lands on your own side. (10.C.1, 10.C.2)

**69. What if the ball hits a player?**
After the serve, if the ball touches a player or anything worn or carried, except
the paddle or a hand below the wrist while it is in contact with the paddle, it is a
fault against that player. This also covers a ball you hit that strikes an opponent
before landing: the shot is a legal return and the opponent is the one who faults.
(10.C, 10.C.3)

**70. What if the ball hits a wall, fence or other permanent object?**
If your hit touches one before bouncing on the court, it is a fault against you. If
a ball has already bounced on your side and you fail to return it before it touches
one, that is a fault against you too. (10.C.4, 10.C.5)

**71. Are double hits and missed swings legal?**
A ball may be hit more than once if it is one continuous stroke in a single
direction by one player. A non-continuous double hit is a fault, and so is both
partners striking the ball. A complete miss leaves the ball live. (10.D, 10.D.1,
10.D.2, 10.E)

**72. What if the ball cracks, softens or breaks mid-rally?**
Play continues to the end of the rally, and the ball is replaced if all players
agree. If all agree a broken or cracked ball affected the outcome, replay; if they
don't all agree, the result stands. A soft or degraded ball never triggers a replay
of the prior rally. (10.G; with a referee, 20.F)

**73. What about injuries, gear problems or things I drop?**
An injury or equipment problem mid-rally does not stop play until the rally ends. If
something you wear or carry lands on your end, the ball stays in play even if it
hits that item. Between rallies you may quickly hydrate, towel off and adjust gear
if you don't disrupt the flow. (10.H–10.K)

**74. Can I distract my opponent?**
No. Distraction means actions uncommon to the game that interfere with an opponent
about to hit, such as loud noises, stomping, or waving the paddle erratically. The
standard rules forbid it; with a referee, it is a fault in the referee's judgment.
(Definitions, 10.F, 20.I.1)

## The non-volley zone

**75. May I stand in the kitchen?**
Yes, any time except while you are volleying. You or anything touching you may
contact the zone as long as you are not in the act of volleying. (11.A)

**76. What makes a kitchen fault while volleying?**
If the volleying player, or anything in contact with them (including their partner),
touches the zone, including its lines, it is a fault. That covers a paddle, hat or
shoe. (11.A.1, 3.A.4.c)

**77. What about momentum?**
If your volley's momentum carries you into the zone or into anything touching it,
including your partner, it is a fault, even after the ball is already dead. The act
of volleying ends only when your follow-through momentum stops. (11.A.2,
Definitions)

**78. Can I volley right after stepping out of the kitchen?**
Only once both feet are completely outside the zone and touching the playing
surface. Volleying before that is a fault. (11.A.3)

**79. Can I hit a ball that has bounced while standing in the kitchen?**
Yes. The restriction is on *volleys*, balls struck out of the air. Players may stand
in the zone for bounced balls. (11.A, Definitions)

**80. Does the kitchen extend upward?**
No. It is two-dimensional and does not extend above the playing surface, so you may
reach over it to volley as long as nothing touching you contacts the zone. (3.A.4.c,
11.A)

**81. Who calls a kitchen fault?**
In casual play, a player calls it on an opponent (non-volley zone faults and
service foot faults are the only faults you may call on opponents), as soon as it is
detected and before the next serve is hit. With a referee, the referee is
responsible for calling non-volley zone faults. (9.B.3, 19.A.1)

## The paddle and the net

**82. May I use two paddles?**
No. Using or carrying more than one paddle during a rally is a fault. (12.A)

**83. Can I hold the paddle in both hands or switch hands?**
Yes, one or both hands, and switching at any time is fine. But you must have
possession when you hit the ball; hitting without the paddle in hand is a fault.
(12.B, 12.B.1)

**84. Can I scoop or carry the ball on my paddle?**
No. Catching or carrying the ball on the paddle during a rally is a fault. (12.C)

**85. Does the ball touching the net end the rally?**
No. The ball stays in play when it hits the net, or the rope or cable on top,
between the posts. A returned ball that crosses and gets caught in the net, hits
net lying on the ground or hits a deflecting net is replayed, as is a rally during a
net-system malfunction. (13.A, 13.A.1, 13.J)

**86. May I hit the ball around, under, or beside the net post?**
Around the *outside* of the post is legal. Between the net and the post, or under
the net, is a fault. (13.C–13.E)

**87. What about hitting the net posts or other net hardware?**
A serve touching any net support is a fault. A return that touches the support on
the hitter's end before going over is a fault. A return that already crossed and then
touches a crossbar or other support within the court boundaries is replayed. (13.B,
13.B.1, 13.B.2)

**88. May I reach over the net?**
Not before the ball has fully crossed to your side: hitting it early is a fault. You
may cross the net plane only immediately after hitting the ball, as part of that
stroke; crossing before the hit, or crossing without hitting the ball, is a fault.
A ball that bounces on your side and spins or blows back over the net may be played
across the net only after it has crossed back, and you must hit it before it bounces
once on your opponent's side. (13.F.1, 13.I.1, 13.I.1.a, 13.I.1.b, 13.I.2)

**89. What if I touch the net or my opponent?**
If you, or anything you wear or carry, touch the net or its support while the ball is
live, or touch an opponent or their end of the court, it is a fault. (13.G.1, 13.H.1)

## Round robins, time-outs and tournaments

**90. How is a round robin decided?**
By matches won. Ties are broken in order: head-to-head record among the tied teams;
best point differential across all games; head-to-head point differential; point
differential against the next-highest team in the standings; most points scored.
Whichever step first breaks the tie ranks all the tied teams. Point differential
and points scored don't apply across pools of unequal size. (15.B.4, 15.B.4.b–f)
**(Tournament)**

**91. What happens if a team withdraws, retires or is forfeited from a round robin?**
Their results do not count toward the standings and they are not eligible for any
playoff or medal match in that event. (Their results still count for ratings.)
(15.B.4.a) **(Tournament)**

**92. What match formats are standard?**
Best two of three to 11; best three of five to 11; one game to 15 or 21; and one
game to 11 only in round robins with six or more teams. All are win-by-two (team
play excepted). (15.C.1) **(Tournament)**

**93. How many time-outs do we get?**
Two standard time-outs per game to 11 or 15, and three in a game to 21, each up to
one minute. They can't be called before the match starts, must be called between
rallies before the next serve (calling after the serve is a fault), and must be
called audibly or by hand signal. A player needing care may request one medical
time-out per match, up to 15 minutes. (21.A, 21.A.1, 21.A.2.a, 21.C) **(Tournament)**

**94. How late can I be to my match?**
Report within 10 minutes of the match being called. Otherwise the referee forfeits
the first game, which is a match forfeit if the match is one game. In multiple-game
matches the cutoff is 15 minutes, for a match forfeit. The director may grant a
delay for extenuating circumstances. (18.F, 18.F.1, 18.F.2) **(Tournament)**

**95. What is the difference between retiring and withdrawing?**
Retirement means stopping a match in progress or in advance; the match goes to the
opponent, earlier results are kept, and a game in progress is recorded at the actual
score with the opponent awarded a winning score. Withdrawal means leaving the whole
bracket before your next match's score call; you're removed from upcoming matches
and their unplayed games are recorded 0–0. (23.A, 23.A.4, 23.B, 23.B.4)
**(Tournament)**

**96. How are forfeits recorded?**
A forfeited game is reported as zero for the forfeiting side (for example 11–0,
15–0 or 21–0), and a forfeited match gets zeroes for all its games. Earlier scores
stay. (22.H, 22.K) **(Tournament)**

**97. Can someone coach me or can I wear earbuds?**
No to both, in competition. Coaching comes only from your partner, except during
time-outs and between games. Headphones, earbuds and other audio-communication
equipment may not be worn or used, except for prescribed hearing aids. (20.G, 18.D)
**(Tournament)**

**98. What happens between games and between matches?**
Between games, sides change ends and initial service for a break of up to two
minutes, and teams may change starting server. Between matches, 10 minutes is
allowed (starting early if everyone agrees). To start a refereed match, a fair
method (such as a coin toss) decides who chooses to serve, receive, or pick an end,
or to defer, and the choice can't be changed. (21.F, 21.G, 17.C.4) **(Tournament)**

## Inclusive play (briefly)

**99. How does the two-bounce allowance work for wheelchair players?**
A wheelchair player may let the ball bounce twice before returning it; the second
bounce can be anywhere on the playing surface, and failing to return it before a
third bounce is a fault. The wheelchair counts as part of the body, and the large
rear wheels, not the small front wheels, are treated like a standing player's legs
for kitchen and serving-area rules. (25.A.4, 25.A.9, 25.A.10)

**100. Who may use the two-bounce allowance in adaptive standing play?**
Eligible adaptive standing players (for example, many above-knee amputees and
players with significant mobility, balance or coordination limits). It must be
declared before the match and used for the whole match, and anyone with any doubt
about eligibility must not use it. Eligibility may be self-assessed. (25.B.1,
25.B.1.a, 25.B.3.a)

**101. What is hybrid doubles?**
A doubles event where each team has one wheelchair player and one standing player,
able-bodied or adaptive. Only eligible adaptive standing players may use the
two-bounce allowance in hybrid play, and they must declare it before the match.
(25.C.1, 25.C.3)

## Where this app's house rules differ from the rulebook

This repository's browser tournament desk is for **casual** events, not sanctioned
ones. Checked against the same 2026 rulebook, these are the differences worth
knowing:

| Topic | Rulebook (2026) | This app |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Round-robin tie-breakers | Head-to-head, then point differential (all games), head-to-head differential, differential against next-highest team, then points scored (15.B.4) | Wins, point margin, then points scored; remaining ties share a place |
| Withdrawal from a round robin | The withdrawn team's results don't count toward standings and it can't reach the playoff (15.B.4.a) | Played games stay and count for opponents; unplayed games become walkovers (a win with no points); the withdrawn entry is unranked and can't qualify |
| Double-elimination decider | If the consolation-bracket winner beats the winners-bracket winner, a tie-breaker match **to 15** decides (15.B.1) | A reset final is played at the event's game target |
| Single game to 11 | Standard only for round robins of six or more teams (15.C.1.d) | Offered for any size as a casual house rule |
| Time-outs, end changes, referees, forfeit scoring | Defined in Part III | Not modelled |

None of these is a bug in a casual event, but an organizer who needs sanctioned
results should follow the rulebook directly.
